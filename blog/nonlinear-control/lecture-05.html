for \(\dot x = Ax\)

  • we can check SISL \(\rightarrow\) \(Re[\lambda_i(A)]\le 0\)
  • AS/ES \(\rightarrow\) \(Re[\lambda_i(A)]< 0\)
  • unstable \(\rightarrow\) \(\exists i, Re[{\lambda_i(A)}] > 0\)

for nonlinear systems \(\dot x = f(x)\), \(x_e=0\):

example: \(\ddot y + y =0\)
\(x_1=y\)
\(x_2=\dot y\)
\(x_e=0\) \[\implies \dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x \] \(\lambda_1 = j, \lambda_2=-j\)

look at energy function: \(V(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2\)
where \(x_1\) is potential and \(x_2\) is kinetic energy and the sum is the total energy of the system

facts:
(a) \(V(x) \le C^2 = \text{ const } \ge 0 \iff x_1^2+x_2^2 \le C^2\)

  • sublevel sets of V: {\(x | V(x) \le C^2\)}
  • sublevel set is a closed, bounded circle of radius C
    (b) \(\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = [\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial V}{\partial x_2}]\) = gradient of \(V\)
  • = \([2x_1,2x_2]\)
  • suppose we take a point on the boundary of the sublevel set, the vector is the gradient
    • the vector is normal to the tangent, pointing out of the circle
    • \(\frac{\partial V }{\partial x}\) is orthogonal to \(V(x)=C^2\) & points in increasing direction of V
      (c) \(\dot x = f(x)\), \(\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x(t))\)
  • the direction of this vector is tangent to the curve
  • shows us the direction that the system will continue moving (motion of system)
    (d) \(V_1, V_2 \in \mathbb{R}^n\)
  • \(\cos(\theta)=\frac{<v_1,v_2>}{||v_1||_2||v_2||_2}\)
  • \(<v_1,v_2>=0\iff\) orthogonal (\(\theta = \pi/2\))
  • \(<v_1,v_2>\space > 0 \iff -90 \degree <\theta <90\degree\)
  • \(<v_1,v_2> \space < 0 \iff \theta > 90 \degree\) or \(\theta < -90 \degree\)
    (e) consider \(<\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},f(x)>\)
  • \(<\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},f(x)> \space =0\iff\) trajectory moves tangent to sublevel set {\(x | V(x) \le C^2\)} which means V(x) remains constant
  • \(<\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},f(x)>\space >0\iff\) trajectories moving towards increasing V
  • \(<\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},f(x)>\space <0\iff\) trajectories moving towards decreasing V
    (f) consider \(\frac{d}{dt}V(x(t))\)
  • \(\frac{d}{dt}V(x(t))=\frac{\partial V }{\partial x }\cdot \frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial x} \cdot \dot x\)
  • = \(<\frac{\partial V }{\partial x}x,f(x)>|_{x(t)}=\dot V(x)\)

back to example:
\[ \dot x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}x, \space x = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ -x_1 \end{bmatrix} \] \(\lambda_1 = j, \lambda_2=-j, V(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2\)
\[\dot V(x)=\begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 & 2x_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ -x_1 \end{bmatrix} =0\]

V is constant along trajectories of the system. \(\implies\) if V is constant, the trajectory evolves along the circles.

example: \[\dot x = \begin{bmatrix} -1/2 & 1 \\ -1 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix} = f(x)\]\[V(x) = x_1^2+x_2^2\]
\[\dot V = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 & 2x_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1/2x_1+x_2 \\ -x_1-1/2x_2 \end{bmatrix} \]
= \(-x_1^2-x_2^2\)
= \(<\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}, f(x)>=-x_1^2-x_2^2<0\) for \(x \not = 0\)
\(V(x)\) decreases along trajectories until \(V(x)=0\), when the trajectory stops

useful math background

  1. def: a subset \(S \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is bounded if \(\exists K < \infty\) s.t. \(\forall x \in S, ||x|| \le K\)
  2. lemma: suppose that \(V:\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is continuous. then, \(\forall C \in \mathbb{R}\), the set of points \(L(c)=\){\(x\in\mathbb{R}^n | V)x_ \le C\)}
    1. proof: we will use the sequence characterization for closed sets. let x(n) be a sequence of points in L(c) (i.e., \(V(x_n) \le C \forall n \ge 1)\) s.t. \(x_n \rightarrow \bar x\).
    2. to show: \(\bar x \in L(c)\) (i.e. \(V(\bar x) \le C\)) . Since V is continuous, \(V(x) = V(\lim x_n) = \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}V(x_n)\).
    3. But, \(\forall n,\space V(x_n) \le C \implies V(\bar x) \le C\)
  3. def: a function V:\(\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_+\) is radially unbounded if \(\forall k_1 < \infty, \space \exists k_2 < \infty\) s.t. \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) with \(||x|| \ge k_2\). it follows that \(V(x) \ge k_1\) (i.e., \(||x|| \ge k_2 \implies V(x) \ge k_1\))
    1. notation: (i) \(V(x) \rightarrow \infty\) as \(||x|| \rightarrow \infty\)
    2. (ii) \(\mathbb{R}_+ = [x \in \mathbb{R} | x \ge 0]\)
  4. lemma: suppose that V:\(\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_+\) is
    1. continuous
    2. \(V(0)=0\)
    3. \(V(x)>0\) for \(x \not = 0\)
    4. \(V(x) \rightarrow \infty\) as \(||x|| \rightarrow \infty\)
      then, \(\forall C \ge 0\), \(L(c) :=[x\in \mathbb{R}^n | V(x) \ge C]\) is closed and bounded.
  5. proof: that L(c) is closed was shown in lemma 2. it suffices to show that L(c) is bounded. fix \(C \ge 0\). if L(c) is NOT bounded, then \(\forall K < \infty, \exists x \in L(c)\) with \(||x|| \ge K.\) that is, \(\forall K < \infty, \exists x\) s.t. \(V(x) \le C\) and \(||x|| \ge K\) which implies that \(V\) is not radially unbounded. hence, \(L(c)\) must be bounded.
  6. def: a subset \(S \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is compact if it is closed and bounded.
    1. note: this definition is not valid for infinite dimensional normed spaces (\(V, ||\cdot ||\))
    2. compact sets have some very nice properties, some of which would be brought out in the following
  7. theorem (weirstrass): suppose that \(f:S\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is continuous and that S is compact. then, f achieves a maximum and a minimum on S, that is, \(\exists s^* \in S\) and \(s_* \in S\) such that \(f(s^*)=\sup_{x \in S}f(x)\) and \(f(s_*)= \inf_{x \in S}f(x)\).
  8. example:
    1. \(S=[0,\infty) \subset \mathbb{R}\) closed but NOT bounded.
      • \(f(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2};\space \inf_{x\in S}f(x) =0\)
    2. \(S = (0,1)\) bounded but NOT closed
      • \(f(x) = x; \inf_{x\in S}f(x)=0\) BUT \(\not \exists x_* \in S\) s.t. \(f(x_*)=0\)
      • \(f(x) = x; \sup_{x\in S}f(x)=1\) BUT \(\not \exists x^* \in S\) s.t. \(f(x^*)=1\)
  9. definition: \(h:S \subset \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m\) is uniformly continuous if \(\forall \varepsilon > 0 \space \exists \delta(\varepsilon)>0\) s.t. \(x,y \in S, ||x \cdot y || < \delta \implies ||h(x) \cdot h(y) || < \varepsilon\)
    1. key point: some \(\delta\) must work for all x and y.

then what is the difference between being continuous and uniformly continuous?

  • h is cont. at y if \(\forall \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0\) s.t. \(\forall x \in B_\delta(y) \implies f(x) \in B_\varepsilon(f(y)) \iff \forall x, ||x-y||<\delta \implies ||f(x)-f(y)||<\varepsilon\)
  1. example: \(h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) by \(x \rightarrow e^x\) (i.e. \(h(x)=e^x\)) is certainly continuous at every point in \(\mathbb{R}\). but, it is NOT uniformly continuous.
  2. theorem: if \(h:S\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m\) is continuous and S is compact, then h is uniformly continuous .
    1. continuity + compactness \(\implies\) unif. continuity
  3. example: \(S=(0,1];h(x)=1/x\) not unif. cont.
    1. however, for \(S=[10^{-3},1]; h(x)=1/x\), IS unif. cont.
  4. def: a function \(h:\mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is bounded from below if \(\exists m>-\infty\) s.t. \(h(x) \ge m \space \forall x \in \mathbb{R}\)
    1. a function \(h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is non-increasing if \(x \ge y \implies h(x) \le h(y)\)
  5. theorem: if \(h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is non-increasing and bounded from below, then \(\exists ! c \in \mathbb{R}\) s.t. \(\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}h(x)=c\) (i.e. \(\forall \epsilon > 0 \exists k < \infty\) s.t. \(\exists x \ge k, |h(x)-c|<\epsilon\))
    1. similarly, if h is non-decreasing and bounded from below, then \(\exists ! c\in \mathbb{R}\) s.t. \(\lim_{x\rightarrow \infty}h(x) = c\).

lyapunov stability theory
\(\dot x = f(x)\) time-invariant
define \(x_e\) as eq. pt. where \(f(x_e)=0\) and \(x_e\) is an isolated eq. pt. if in addition \(\exists a>0\) s.t. \(x_e\) is the only eq. pt. in \(B_a(x_e)\) .

  • for this pt. to be stable in the sense of lyapunov (SISL):
  • def: let \(x_e\) be an eq. point of \(\dot x = f(x)\). s.t. \(\exists \rho >0\) for which \(x_0 \in B_\rho(x_e)\implies x(t,x_0)\) exists for all time and is unique.
  • (a) \(x_e\) is SISL if \(\exists \varepsilon > 0, \exists \delta > 0,\) s.t. \(||x_0-x_e|| < \delta \implies ||x(t,x_0)-x_e||<\varepsilon \forall t \ge 0\)
    • \(\iff x_0 \in B_\delta (x_e) \implies x(t,x_0) \in B)_\varepsilon(x_e)\)
  • \(\Psi: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{C}_n[0,\infty),x_0 \rightarrow x(t,x_0)\)
    • \(\Psi\) is cont. at \(x_e \iff \exists \varepsilon >0,\exists \delta >0\)
    • s.t. \(\exists x_0 \in B_\delta(x_e) \implies \Psi(x_0) \in B_\varepsilon(\Psi(x_e))\)
